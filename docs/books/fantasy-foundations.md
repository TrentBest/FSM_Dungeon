# Fantasy Foundations

**Library:** FSM_Dungeon Fantasy Library  
**LOD:** 1 — general framework  
**Status:** authored foundation; deliberately system-neutral

## Purpose

Fantasy is not a list of races, monsters, spells, and treasure. It is a world in which extraordinary possibilities have consequences. The setting becomes believable when geography, ecology, societies, technology, belief, history, and individual choices constrain and influence one another.

This book establishes a reusable framework for original fantasy settings. It does not prescribe one canonical world, cosmology, pantheon, magic system, or ruleset.

## 1. Build from a world, not a monster list

Start with:
- physical geography, climate, watersheds, seasons, and natural resources;
- ecological relationships, migration, predation, disease, and environmental change;
- peoples, settlement patterns, languages, family structures, institutions, and exchange;
- historical events, migrations, disasters, discoveries, conflicts, and unresolved claims;
- extraordinary forces such as magic, spirits, relics, planar phenomena, or ancient technologies;
- what ordinary people know, what specialists believe, and what remains hidden.

A setting should work between adventures. Food is grown or gathered, tools are repaired, children learn, travelers need shelter, communities negotiate, and institutions make decisions.

## 2. Peoples are populations, not character classes

A people may share ancestry or a broad cultural history without sharing one personality, occupation, political position, religion, or moral alignment. Describe variation across geography, generations, communities, households, and individuals.

For each people, author:
- self-identification and names used by others;
- origins and competing origin accounts;
- physical variation only where useful and responsibly grounded in the setting;
- languages, communication, art, education, and memory;
- family, household, community, governance, and institutions;
- livelihoods, exchange, craft, technology, and resource dependencies;
- internal disagreements, reform movements, marginalized groups, and alliances;
- relationships with neighbors, including trade, interdependence, conflict, and misunderstanding.

No people should be reduced to a single moral or occupational stereotype.

## 3. Creatures belong to ecologies

A creature entry should explain more than how dangerous it is. Record habitat, range, diet, lifecycle, reproduction, social behavior, predators, competitors, environmental effects, and interactions with settlements. Distinguish solitary individuals from packs, colonies, herds, nests, and migrating populations.

A dangerous creature may be defending young, responding to habitat loss, following prey, guarding a resource, or acting under unusual conditions. A creature's presence should be explainable.

## 4. Magic and the extraordinary

A setting may support many forms of extraordinary influence. Define them through observable constraints:
- what can be affected;
- what is required to cause an effect;
- range, duration, scale, precision, and repeatability;
- costs, risks, side effects, and failure modes;
- who can learn or access the capability;
- how communities adapt their laws, economies, infrastructure, and beliefs.

Do not let magic erase every meaningful problem unless that is an intentional premise with consequences of its own. A reliable healing art changes medicine and warfare; reliable long-distance communication changes politics and trade; effortless transport changes settlement patterns.

SURPS owns the rules-facing capability and resolution semantics. This book records setting assumptions and consequences.

## 5. Places have histories

A location is more than a map label. Describe why it formed, who used it, what changed, and why its present condition exists. For underground and built environments, FSM_Dungeon can represent spatial topology, formation, construction history, environmental conditions, and traversability.

A ruin should have builders, purpose, materials, access, maintenance history, abandonment causes, later occupants, and evidence. A lair should have an ecological and behavioral explanation. A settlement should have water, food, shelter, trade, waste, movement, governance, and relationships with its surroundings.

## 6. Conflict and consequence

Conflict may be political, familial, ecological, economic, religious, personal, exploratory, or military. Warfare is one category, not the default explanation for every disagreement. When organized violence or fortification is central, raWWar owns the warfare doctrine and its specialist manuals.

Every major conflict should identify:
- incompatible interests or pressures;
- the actors and what each believes;
- resources, constraints, and asymmetric information;
- nonviolent and violent options;
- immediate effects and delayed consequences;
- people who bear the costs but do not control the decision.

## 7. Story is a changing world observed by participants

A prepared story is not a mandatory sequence. Authors establish a situation, actors, motives, pressures, places, clues, and possible consequences. Players make choices; the world responds; the resulting events become the campaign's history.

StoryBuilder owns this lifecycle. It should reference this book and its entries by stable identity and pinned revision, then author campaigns, adventures, quests, scenes, encounters, and sessions over that shared foundation.

## 8. Evidence and knowledge

Every setting fact should be classifiable as one of:
- established world fact;
- attributed historical claim;
- common knowledge;
- specialist knowledge;
- rumor or legend;
- disputed interpretation;
- unknown or intentionally unrevealed.

The narrator, GM, character, and player may have different knowledge. The existence of an entry in an authoring library does not mean every in-world observer knows it.

## 9. A practical setting worksheet

For a new region, answer:
1. What is physically here?
2. What lives here, and how does it survive?
3. Who lives here, and how do they sustain ordinary life?
4. What do they need from elsewhere?
5. What happened here before the present?
6. What is changing now?
7. What do different observers believe is happening?
8. What can player choices change?
9. Which owning repository supplies the rules or simulation?
10. What would make this place recognizable without relying on a familiar franchise?

## Ownership

This is a setting-authoring framework. It does not implement creature AI, combat, magic resolution, physics, or campaign execution. Those remain with SURPS, Physics, other specialized providers, and StoryBuilder as appropriate.
