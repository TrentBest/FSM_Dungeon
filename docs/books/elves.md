# Elves: Peoples of the Long Memory

**Library:** FSM_Dungeon Fantasy Library  
**LOD:** 1 — people and cultural framework  
**Status:** original setting proposal; not universal fantasy canon

## Premise

The word *elf* is a broad family of names used by neighboring peoples for several related communities. It does not describe one kingdom, one appearance, one temperament, or one political purpose. The communities gathered under this name share a disputed deep history and a set of old cultural connections, but have developed in different environments and made different choices.

Some communities maintain long-lived archives; others distrust written records. Some live in forest settlements, others in river cities, highland terraces, coastal enclaves, or among other peoples. Longevity, where present in a particular setting, changes institutions and personal experience; it does not make every elf wise, patient, wealthy, or politically powerful.

This is an original, adaptable setting proposal. Names, dates, physical traits, and history can be revised by the setting's author.

## 1. Identity and variation

Use *elf* as a broad exonym or family label until a setting defines the communities' own names. Avoid assuming that every elf identifies with the same ancestry, language, government, or shared destiny.

A community entry should record:
- its self-name and neighboring names;
- local history and migration;
- language and communication traditions;
- family, household, education, and care;
- governance and dispute resolution;
- livelihoods, trade, craft, and resource dependencies;
- beliefs, art, ceremony, and ordinary recreation;
- internal disagreement and changing generations;
- relations with other elf communities and neighboring peoples.

Individuals may embrace, reinterpret, or reject inherited traditions. An elf can be a farmer, navigator, mason, physician, parent, merchant, student, soldier, poet, or none of those labels.

## 2. The Long Memory

The Long Memory is not a magical hive mind. It is the collective, imperfect record of remembered events, oral testimony, songs, maps, family accounts, preserved objects, and written archives.

Its central tension is evidentiary: a record can survive longer than its author, but survival does not make it true. Archives may preserve contradictory accounts, omit marginalized voices, or be deliberately altered. A person who remembers an event may disagree with the institution that claims to speak for it.

This gives StoryBuilder a natural foundation for:
- historical claims with source provenance;
- multiple timelines and disputed chronology;
- observer-specific knowledge;
- scenes that portray the same event from different perspectives;
- continuity review without automatically declaring one account correct.

## 3. Communities of the first setting proposal

These are optional starting communities, not fixed biological subraces.

### The Aelvarin of the Greenward

Forest and woodland-edge communities whose settlements are distributed along watersheds and old migration corridors. Their architecture uses living materials alongside stone, woven structures, and maintained paths. They disagree over whether the forest should be managed as a shared commons or protected through restricted stewardship.

### The Thalen of the River Glass

River and estuary communities whose histories are recorded through navigational charts, trade agreements, songs, and public markers. Their identity is shaped by movement and exchange rather than isolation. They are often intermediaries, but their ports are not politically neutral.

### The Vael Turog of the High Quiet

Highland communities living among exposed ridges, terraces, and sheltered valleys. Their engineering traditions emphasize water capture, wind exposure, and route maintenance. Their archives include practical records of climate and landslide history, which are sometimes more contested than their royal genealogies.

## 4. Relationships with other peoples

Elves are not inherently superior, secretive, benevolent, or hostile. Their long histories may create both useful perspective and institutional inertia. A community can be a reliable ally in one matter and an adversary in another.

For each relationship, record:
- the specific communities or institutions involved;
- what each side wants and fears;
- trade and resource dependencies;
- historical events and whose account is being used;
- current agreements, disputes, and points of contact;
- what ordinary residents know versus what diplomats or historians know.

Dwarven and elven relations should not be reduced to an eternal species feud. Shared engineering, trade, competing land claims, old alliances, cultural misunderstandings, and individual friendships can coexist.

## 5. Storyline seed: The Archive Beneath the Roots

**Campaign premise:** An old archive is discovered beneath a forest settlement after a seasonal river changes course. The archive predates the oldest surviving local records. Its sealed chambers contain evidence that a celebrated founding account is incomplete—not necessarily false, but missing the people who made the settlement possible.

**Opening situation:** A landslip exposes a worked-stone threshold. A local surveyor reports unfamiliar construction marks. The settlement council wants the site secured; a family archive keeper wants it studied; a neighboring community claims the route crosses an old boundary; and a group of young residents wants public access.

**Central questions:**
- Who built the archive, and for whom?
- Why was it sealed?
- Which records were preserved, copied, altered, or excluded?
- What does the river's changed course reveal about the original settlement?
- Does revealing the archive repair an injustice or create a new one?
- Who has authority to interpret a shared past?

**Possible developments (not a prescribed sequence):**
1. The party investigates the exposed threshold and gathers competing accounts.
2. A safe route into the archive requires understanding its water and ventilation systems.
3. The first records contradict a public founding story, but the evidence is incomplete.
4. A neighboring community reveals a parallel oral history.
5. The party discovers that the archive was designed to preserve testimony from several peoples, not to crown one lineage.
6. The campaign's resolution depends on what the participants choose to publish, protect, return, or leave undisclosed.

**Possible outcomes:** public archive, shared stewardship, private family custody, a negotiated research compact, a sealed site, or a contested disclosure. Each creates different consequences for trust, politics, and future access.

## 6. StoryBuilder composition notes

The storyline should become a StoryBuilder campaign, not a static branching novel. Model:
- the settlement, archive, river, and neighboring communities as stable places and organizations;
- the founding account and alternative histories as attributed claims with source records;
- the landslip and discoveries as proposed events until accepted;
- each character's knowledge separately from GM knowledge;
- the opening investigation as an adventure containing quests and scenes;
- possible revelations as contingencies, not mandatory outcomes;
- player decisions and GM rulings as durable campaign history.

Pin the exact sourcebook revision used by the campaign. Campaign-specific changes belong to that campaign, not back into this shared book.

## 7. Rules and environment boundaries

SURPS defines elf ontology, capabilities, needs, behavior, relationships, and action resolution. FSM_Dungeon supplies any relevant underground topology, environmental conditions, and site history. Physics owns physical calculations; Singularity City owns general civic place/resident relationships where used; StoryBuilder owns the evolving campaign record.

This book intentionally supplies no mechanical statistics or universal elf rules.
