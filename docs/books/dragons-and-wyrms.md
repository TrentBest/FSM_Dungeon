## Purpose

Dragons are often treated as a single creature type whose only meaningful dimensions are size, age, and destructive power. This book treats draconic life as a broad family of organisms and cultures whose forms, needs, intelligence, and ecological roles vary by lineage and setting.

A dragon is not automatically evil, solitary, wealthy, magical, or interested in conquest. Those are possible traits of particular individuals or traditions, not universal biological laws. A world may contain animal-like drakes, highly social wyrms, sapient elder lineages, or none of these. The author must decide which possibilities exist.

This sourcebook supplies reusable natural-history and worldbuilding descriptions. SURPS owns entity ontology, capabilities, motivations, behavior, and resolution. Physics owns physical calculations. StoryBuilder owns campaign events and continuity.

## 1. A family, not a stat block

For authoring purposes, distinguish three broad working categories. They are descriptive labels, not a closed taxonomy:

- **Drakes:** generally smaller-bodied draconic animals. Their intelligence and sociality vary. Some are solitary ambush predators; others are herd browsers, cliff nesters, or cooperative scavengers.
- **Wyrms:** large, long-lived draconic organisms. Their size and lifespan create significant ecological effects, but do not by themselves establish sapience or supernatural ability.
- **Elder wyrms:** rare individuals or lineages whose cognition, memory, social organization, and extraordinary capabilities may place them among the world's major political or historical actors.

A setting may rename, subdivide, combine, or reject these categories. Record evidence and uncertainty rather than forcing every sighting into a known class.

## 2. Body plans and adaptations

Draconic body plans should follow the environment and life history selected by the author. Possible adaptations include:
- powered flight, gliding, cliff launching, or complete loss of flight;
- limbs specialized for climbing, swimming, digging, grasping, or terrestrial movement;
- insulating scales, heat exchange structures, sensory crests, or pressure-sensitive membranes;
- specialized jaws, teeth, beaks, or feeding structures;
- egg-laying, live birth, communal nesting, or other explicitly defined reproduction;
- chemical, thermal, electrical, sonic, or magical defenses where the setting establishes them.

Do not assume a large flying animal can fly merely because it has wings. Flight, heat production, breath weapons, armor, and other extraordinary traits need internally consistent setting explanations. Physics can assess physical plausibility; fantasy premises may deliberately depart from real-world constraints, but should identify the departure and its consequences.

## 3. Habitat and range

A draconic population requires a viable habitat. Record:
- nesting and shelter requirements;
- food and water sources;
- thermal and seasonal constraints;
- territory size and overlap;
- migration and dispersal routes;
- juvenile survival pressures;
- competition, predation, parasites, and disease;
- relationships with settlements and other species.

A dragon's lair should be a place it can reach, occupy, and sustain. A mountain cavern may offer thermal stability and defensible nesting ledges; a marsh may support abundant prey but impose different movement constraints. A lair's architecture should reflect the occupant's body, behavior, history, and available materials.

## 4. Feeding, hoards, and resource concentration

A hoard is not an obligatory biological feature. If a lineage accumulates objects, define why:
- reflective objects attract mates or support display;
- metal or mineral deposits are consumed or used in nesting;
- objects carry cultural, historical, or ritual significance;
- a territory's occupants collect tribute;
- an individual preserves evidence, memories, or leverage;
- the supposed hoard is an outsider's interpretation of an archive, cache, or resource reserve.

A large concentration of valuables changes local behavior. It may attract scavengers, prospectors, rival claimants, thieves, governments, or conservation efforts. The consequences matter more than the trope.

## 5. Intelligence and society

Do not infer intelligence from age or size. A lineage may exhibit:
- instinctive but complex animal behavior;
- learned traditions within a population;
- social learning and intergenerational knowledge;
- individual sapience;
- language, diplomacy, law, art, and institutions;
- radically different forms of cognition that outsiders misunderstand.

Where dragons are persons, describe households, kinship, disagreement, care, labor, exchange, memory, political structures, and relationships with other peoples. Avoid making every member of a lineage share one ideology or personality.

Where they are animals, do not give them human political motives merely to create a plot. Their needs and learned behavior can still create meaningful conflict.

## 6. Signs and field evidence

A field record may include:
- tracks, claw marks, shed scales, feathers, or other biological traces;
- feeding remains and prey distribution;
- nesting material, eggshell, or juvenile tracks;
- heat, airflow, chemical residue, or disturbed sediment;
- altered vegetation, water quality, or local animal behavior;
- repeated routes, territorial markings, and seasonal observations;
- witness accounts with date, location, distance, and reliability.

Distinguish a confirmed observation from an inference. A burned structure does not prove a dragon caused it. A large track does not establish species, age, or intent.

## 7. Relationships with peoples

Possible relationships include avoidance, predation, domestication, mutualism, worship, scholarship, trade, negotiated territory, protection, exploitation, and war. None should be assumed universal.

A community living near a draconic nesting range may develop seasonal closures, warning systems, negotiated access, specialized architecture, or stories that encode practical safety knowledge. Another community may dispute those practices or benefit from the risks.

When a dragon is an intelligent political actor, raWWar may own warfare doctrine if organized conflict becomes central. SURPS owns the participants and their rules-facing behavior. StoryBuilder records the actual choices and consequences.

## 8. Story situations, not mandatory encounters

Useful situations include:
- a nesting ground is threatened by a new road or mine;
- a juvenile has been separated from its population;
- a supposed monster attack is actually a territorial warning;
- an old hoard contains records that establish a disputed boundary;
- a settlement's protective pact has become exploitative;
- two neighboring lineages disagree over migration access;
- a dragon's absence causes an ecological cascade;
- an elder wyrm's testimony conflicts with the surviving archive.

Each situation should support multiple approaches and outcomes. Combat is possible, not compulsory.

## 9. LOD progression

- **LOD 0:** rumor, fossil, disputed sighting, or evidence of a draconic presence.
- **LOD 1:** broad lineage description, range, ecology, and common signs.
- **LOD 2:** regional population, habitat, nest/lair, relationships, and attributed local history.
- **LOD 3+:** named individual, lineage, persistent population, territory, and campaign-specific history.

Higher LOD adds information; it does not make every observer aware of it.

## Ownership and canon

This is an original, adaptable sourcebook proposal. It defines no combat statistics, spell effects, creature AI, or universal dragon cosmology. Those belong to the appropriate owning domains. No entry becomes canonical in a particular campaign merely because it appears in this library; StoryBuilder pins the edition and records campaign adoption or adaptation.
