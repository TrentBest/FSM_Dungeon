# Ruins and Lost Places: Reading the Layers of a World

**Library:** FSM_Dungeon Fantasy Library  
**Provisional sourcebook ID:** `fsm-dungeon.fantasy.ruins-and-lost-places`  
**Entry IDs:** `place.ruin`, `place.buried-settlement`, `place.sealed-complex`  
**LOD:** 1 — reusable place-authoring framework  
**Edition:** 0.1.0

## Purpose

A ruin is not a dungeon-shaped container for encounters and treasure. It is the surviving evidence of a place that once served a purpose, was inhabited or maintained, changed, and entered a different relationship with its surroundings.

This book provides a reusable framework for authoring ruins, abandoned settlements, buried complexes, collapsed infrastructure, and places whose original purpose is uncertain. It does not prescribe a single lost empire or universal ancient civilization.

FSM_Dungeon owns spatial topology, underground environmental structure, construction and formation provenance, and environmental state. Physics owns physical substrate and validated calculations. SURPS owns inhabitants and their ontology. StoryBuilder owns campaign use, choices, and event history.

## 1. The life of a place

A useful ruin has a causal sequence:

1. **Origin:** natural formation, construction, excavation, biological growth, or a combination.
2. **Purpose:** habitation, production, storage, transport, worship, research, defense, burial, administration, or another use.
3. **Operation:** who used it, how it was supplied, maintained, governed, and accessed.
4. **Change:** expansion, repair, conflict, environmental shift, ownership change, or repurposing.
5. **Disruption:** abandonment, catastrophe, gradual decline, deliberate sealing, or successful transformation.
6. **Afterlife:** scavenging, reuse, occupation, erosion, flooding, burial, ecological succession, or preservation.
7. **Present condition:** what remains, what functions, what is dangerous, and what is misunderstood.

A ruin can be partly active. One wing may be abandoned while another remains inhabited or maintained.

## 2. Purpose leaves a spatial signature

The original purpose should be visible in the arrangement of spaces:
- storage areas need access, protection, and environmental conditions appropriate to their contents;
- workshops need material flow, work surfaces, waste handling, and ventilation;
- residences need privacy, sanitation, light, water, and movement;
- transport facilities need routes, loading areas, turning space, and connections;
- defensive works need observation, access control, supply, and protected circulation;
- archives need environmental protection, cataloguing, and retrieval practices;
- ceremonial spaces need approach, gathering capacity, and meaningful spatial relationships.

The layout need not be intact. Collapses, later construction, and environmental changes can obscure the original plan. Preserve evidence that allows a careful observer to reconstruct competing hypotheses.

## 3. Materials and construction evidence

Record materials, joining methods, tool marks, wear, repairs, and sources where known. Distinguish:
- original construction;
- later additions;
- emergency repairs;
- deliberate alteration;
- scavenger damage;
- natural weathering and decay;
- modern restoration or excavation.

Do not label a structure ancient solely because it looks unusual. Age and provenance should be attributed to evidence, a reliable record, or an explicitly uncertain inference.

Structural safety must not be inferred from appearance. A visually intact arch may be unstable; a collapsed passage may conceal a stable void. Real-world safety claims require engineering evidence and competent review.

## 4. Abandonment is an explanation to investigate

Possible causes include:
- water or resource failure;
- migration or economic relocation;
- disease or demographic decline;
- political change;
- deliberate evacuation;
- conflict or siege;
- fire, flood, landslide, subsidence, or other environmental event;
- loss of a critical route or external dependency;
- gradual adaptation into a different use.

Avoid a single convenient catastrophe unless the setting supports it. A settlement may have declined through several overlapping causes. Different witnesses can preserve conflicting accounts.

## 5. The present ecosystem

After abandonment, a place becomes part of its environment. Consider:
- water entering or leaving the structure;
- airflow, temperature, humidity, and light;
- mineral deposition, corrosion, and material decay;
- plant, fungal, and animal colonization;
- sediment movement and blocked routes;
- new food sources and nesting opportunities;
- contaminants, toxins, pathogens, and unstable materials;
- the effects of repeated human or creature entry.

An inhabitant's presence should have a reason. A lair may use a warm chamber, a dry nesting shelf, or a reliable water source. Scavengers may follow the remains of earlier occupants. A group may occupy a ruin because it controls a route rather than because it values the ruin itself.

## 6. Evidence, interpretation, and hidden truth

For every significant claim, track its epistemic status:
- directly observed;
- measured or surveyed;
- supported by multiple independent traces;
- attributed to a named source;
- common local belief;
- specialist interpretation;
- disputed hypothesis;
- deliberate deception;
- unknown.

The author or GM may know the actual history while characters see only fragments. Do not collapse these layers into a single omniscient description when preparing player-facing material.

Useful evidence includes inscriptions, tool marks, repairs, food remains, wear patterns, burial practices, water deposits, discarded goods, route geometry, and changes in local ecology. Evidence should support more than one hypothesis when the mystery is meant to remain open.

## 7. Access, hazards, and choices

A ruin may contain:
- blocked, flooded, or unstable routes;
- vertical transitions, shafts, ledges, and chasms;
- poor air, extreme temperatures, or limited visibility;
- active machinery or systems;
- protected archives or sealed chambers;
- living occupants with their own needs and boundaries;
- hazards created by prior exploration.

Record what is known, what is inferred, and what requires specialist inspection. Dungeon can model topology and environmental conditions; it does not certify a real structure as safe.

Design access as a set of meaningful choices. A blocked main entrance may leave a difficult but viable route. A sealed archive may be opened through negotiation, research, repair, or force. A dangerous shortcut should have a comprehensible trade-off.

## 8. Ruins and stories

Story situations should arise from the site's history and present relationships:
- two communities have different claims to the same place;
- restoration would destroy evidence needed to understand it;
- an active occupant is mistaken for the cause of the original abandonment;
- a water system is still serving a settlement downstream;
- a sealed room contains records that undermine a respected local history;
- scavenging provides vital income but accelerates structural loss;
- the ruin is not abandoned at all—it is maintained by people who avoid notice.

A prepared site should describe pressures, actors, evidence, and possible consequences, not a required sequence of rooms and fights.

## 9. LOD progression

- **LOD 0:** a place is rumored, named in a record, or indicated by remote evidence.
- **LOD 1:** broad location, likely origin, known history, and visible condition.
- **LOD 2:** spatial topology, construction phases, environmental state, inhabitants, access, and evidence.
- **LOD 3+:** a named persistent site with surveyed spaces, stable identifiers, observed changes, and campaign history.

A generic ruin template is not a named ruin. A named ruin should have stable identity and provenance.

## 10. Site authoring worksheet

1. What is the site's stable identity and location?
2. Who or what formed or built it?
3. What purpose did it serve?
4. How was it supplied, accessed, and maintained?
5. What changed during its active life?
6. Why did its use change or end?
7. What happened after abandonment?
8. What survives and what is missing?
9. Who occupies, uses, claims, or studies it now?
10. What evidence supports each historical claim?
11. What do different observers believe?
12. What choices can change its future?

## Ownership and canon

This framework defines no universal trap rules, creature behavior, combat mechanics, or structural safety guarantees. Domain owners supply those capabilities. StoryBuilder attaches a pinned edition, selects a named site or template, and records campaign-specific discoveries and changes without silently rewriting shared sourcebook history.
